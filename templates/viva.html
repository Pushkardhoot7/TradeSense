TradeSense Capstone Viva Defense & Core Q&A

A structured walkthrough designed for evaluation: explains why discrete mathematics provides superior interpretability compared to unconstrained black-box ML models in finance.

Question 1 of 12 Core Problem Statement

What is the central problem TradeSense solves?

Traditional quantitative finance relies heavily on Markowitz mean-variance optimization, which is notoriously sensitive to estimation errors and acts as a mathematical "black box". TradeSense replaces unconstrained optimization with discrete structural constraints: graph network sparsification, partial orders (Posets), and vertex coloring to build transparent, explainable, and robust portfolios.

Question 2 of 12 Graph Theory

Why do we represent the stock market as a graph G = (V, E)?

Stocks do not move in isolation; systemic risk propagates through market channels. By defining stocks as vertices $V$ and correlations $r \ge \theta$ as edges $E$, we capture the topology of co-movements. Central vertices (high degree) represent systemic bellwethers, while disconnected components indicate isolated risk clusters.

Question 3 of 12 Graph Coloring

How does Welsh-Powell graph coloring guarantee diversification?

By mathematical definition, no two adjacent vertices share the same color. Since an edge exists between two stocks only if their historical correlation $r \ge \theta$, stocks in the same color class are an independent set: their correlation is strictly bounded below $\theta$. Selecting one stock per color group guarantees low historical co-movement exposure.

Question 4 of 12 Partially Ordered Sets

What is the dominance relation, and why is it a Partial Order?

Stock $A$ dominates stock $B$ ($A \succeq B$) if $\text{Return}(A) \ge \text{Return}(B)$ and $\text{Risk}(A) \le \text{Risk}(B)$. It is a partial order because it is reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive. Because two stocks may have higher return but also higher risk, not all pairs are comparable — thus it is a partial order, not a total order.

Question 5 of 12 Hasse Diagram

What is transitive reduction, and how does it create the Hasse diagram?

If $A \succeq B$ and $B \succeq C$, then $A \succeq C$ by transitivity. Retaining the direct edge $(A, C)$ creates visual clutter and redundancy. Transitive reduction removes all implied composite edges, leaving only the cover relation. The maximal elements at the top of the Hasse DAG constitute the Pareto-optimal frontier.

Question 6 of 12 Combinatorics

Why use Combinatorics C(n, k) rather than continuous weights?

Continuous quadratic programming often yields fractional, micro-weighted holdings (e.g. 0.4% in 40 stocks), incurring high transaction fees and rebalancing friction. Discrete combinatorics $C(n,k)$ evaluates equal-weighted portfolios of a manageable size (e.g., $k=3$ or $k=4$), ensuring practical real-world execution.
